The TL;DR on Fractals

A fractal is an geometric shape that contains infinite recursion and detail.

Imagine zooming into a rugged coastline or a snowflake. No matter how close you look, the structure never simplifies — instead, you discover smaller copies of the exact same patterns repeating infinitely. This property is known as self-similarity.

While traditional geometry deals with clean integer dimensions (a 1D line, a 2D square, a 3D cube), fractals occupy **fractional dimensions**. A highly folded fractal curve might have a dimension of 1.26, packing infinite length into a finite area!

In computer graphics, we render three-dimensional fractals using a process called **raymarching**. Unlike classic polygons, we shoot virtual rays from the camera for each pixel and calculate the mathematical distance to the fractal surface, stepping along the ray until we hit the infinite boundary.

Where Are They in Real Life?

Fractal architecture is the signature language of nature.

  • ✦ Trees and Leaves: branches split off into twigs, which split off into leaf veins, mimicking the same geometric expansion rules at every level.
  • ✦ Lungs and Blood Vessels: our circulatory and respiratory systems branch fractally, maximizing the surface area for oxygen exchange in a compact volume.
  • ✦ Coastlines: the famous "Coastline Paradox" shows that measuring a coast with smaller rulers yields an infinitely longer perimeter because of fractal cragginess.
  • ✦ River Networks: drainage basins converge in tributary branches that form perfect fractal structures when viewed from orbit.
  • ✦ Snowflakes & Crystals: water molecules crystallizing form complex self-similar dendritic structures under microscopic bounds.

The Mathematics of the Infinite

Benoit Mandelbrot and the revolution of fractal geometry.

For centuries, mathematicians dismissed self-similar, continuous-but-nowhere-differentiable equations (like the Weierstrass function or the Koch snowflake) as "mathematical monsters" that had no relevance to physical reality.

In 1979, IBM researcher Benoit Mandelbrot used the power of early computers to visualize these equations. By plotting the simple boundary of numbers that remain stable under iteration, he discovered the famous **Mandelbrot Set** — showing that infinite complexity can arise from a simple formula.

In 2009, mathematical explorers on the Fractal Forums extended this 2D boundary into three dimensions, creating the **Mandelbulb**, a stunning 3D shadow of Mandelbrot's complex number dynamics.
